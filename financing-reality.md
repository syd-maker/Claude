## 1. How lenders will see you

Self-employed borrowers are qualified on the **net income from the 2-year tax return
average** — not gross revenue, not bank deposits. Your Schedule C bottom line after
expenses is the number. Two years of personal and business returns are required, and
conventional DTI caps run about **43–45%**, stretching to 50% with strong compensating
factors.

Combined documented income: **$47,500/yr = $3,958/mo.**

Maximum total monthly debt at 43% DTI: **$1,702.** That ceiling covers *everything* —
mortgage, taxes, insurance, car payments, credit cards, student loans.

### The number that actually tells the story

Monroe County insurance runs **$8,000–20,000+/yr** on a $600k–1M property, and Keys flood
insurance adds **$3,000–15,000+/yr** depending on the elevation certificate. Combined with
property tax:

> **Taxes and insurance alone on a $750k Keys property = $18,000–30,250/yr.
> That is 38–64% of your entire combined gross income, before one dollar of mortgage
> principal, before food, before gas.**

Those carrying costs ($1,500–2,521/mo) **exceed your entire debt capacity of $1,702/mo.**
Even if someone handed you the house free and clear, the insurance and tax bill alone would
sit at the edge of what your income supports.

Working backwards from what you can actually carry: after low-end Keys taxes and insurance,
about **$300/mo** remains for principal and interest — a **~$47,000 loan**, or roughly a
**$58,000 purchase**. That is the honest number today.

## 3. This specific property is worse, not better

Separate from income: **lenders will not write a conventional mortgage on uninhabitable
structures.** Fannie/Freddie, FHA, and VA all require the property be habitable and meet
minimum property standards. You told me none of the buildings are livable.

So this property is **not conventionally financeable at any income level** until the
structures are replaced. The remaining options:

| Path | Reality |
|---|---|
| Cash | Not available |
| Hard money / bridge | 10–14% interest, 30–35% down, 12–24 month term. On a $700k purchase that's $70–95k/yr in interest alone |
| Construction loan | Requires *more* income documentation, contractor bids, 20–25% down |
| **Seller financing** | **The realistic one — see §5** |

## 4. What a cosigner actually signs up for

A cosigner does not top you up. They must qualify for the **entire debt on their own
income**, while still carrying their own housing costs. Practically that means someone
earning **$180,000+** with low existing debt, willing to attach their credit and assets to a
distressed, uninhabitable property in a hurricane zone, for an indefinite term.

That is a very large ask, and it is real risk to them, not a formality. If such a person
exists in your life, the conversation to have is about **partnership** (§5.3), not
cosigning — at least a partner gets equity for the risk.

## 5. Five paths that could actually work

### 5.1 Seller financing — pursue this first
The property's un-financeability is your leverage. The seller's buyer pool is limited to
cash buyers and developers, which is small. A seller carrying paper — 10–20% down,
interest-only or low amortization, 5–10 year balloon — solves your problem and can suit them
too (spread capital gains, earn interest). **Ask directly. This is the highest-probability
path to a 2027 close.**

### 5.2 Sell one building right to fund the rest
You said you'd rather not, and I understand why. But hold it up to the light: three rights at
~$300k each is ~$900k of entitlement value on a property you cannot otherwise develop.
**Selling one could fund the down payment, the demolition, the remediation, and the start of
construction** — leaving you two rights: your home plus one income unit.

Three rights you can't build on are worth less than two you can. This may be the only
genuinely self-funding path, and it deserves real analysis rather than reflex.

### 5.3 Equity partner
You bring the vision, the marketing, the deal, and the operating business. Someone else
brings cash. You take a minority position and own part of something real instead of all of
nothing. Given your marketing skill, this is more plausible than a cosigner.

### 5.4 Option the property — best immediate move
Instead of buying now, negotiate a **purchase option**: pay a modest sum for the exclusive
right to buy at a fixed price for 12–24 months. That locks the price, buys time to verify the
building rights, raise capital, and get plans approved — **without needing the money today.**
Cheap, low-risk, and well suited to exactly your situation.

### 5.5 Raise documented income
The unglamorous one, and the one that fixes everything else. Going from $47.5k to $120k+
combined documented over 18–24 months changes every number above. Note that your income
problem and your business problem are **the same problem** — see §7.

## 6. Your three specific questions

### "Could I truly just do a trailer?"
**No.** Living in an RV or travel trailer during the home-building process is not permitted
in Marathon, and recreational equipment may not be used for living or sleeping purposes on
residentially zoned lots. This closes the "camp cheap on site while building" plan, which
matters — it was probably load-bearing in your mental model. Budget for housing somewhere
else during construction.

### "How much for a lazy river?"
Wrong phase, and probably wrong concept. `[A]` A residential lazy-river-style feature runs
**$150k–500k+**; commercial scale runs **$500k–$3M+**. In the Keys add flood-zone
construction, 24/7 pump electricity, and Keys water costs.

The bigger problem is regulatory: **if paying guests use it, it's a public swimming pool**
under Florida DOH rules — permitting, fencing, water testing, safety requirements, and a
significant liability and insurance load on a business whose insurance is already your
largest cost.

And it fights your own concept. A lazy river turns a contemplative butterfly-and-bird
painting garden into a water park: chlorine, noise, wet guests, crowds. Those two experiences
do not share a property well. **Park this indefinitely.**

### "Clean up paths, replace the wood, a few other steps"

Materially less with the barter arrangement (§7). **But note the sequencing problem: you
cannot spend any of this until you own the property.** Landscaping is a Phase 2 question and
you're currently stuck on Phase 1.

## 7. The two things you got right

**The barter idea is genuinely good.** You have at-cost labor access and real marketing
skill; a landscaper gets a showcase property, a transformation story, and content. Structure
it properly: written scope on both sides, both sides valued explicitly, defined deliverables
and timelines. **Note that bartered services are taxable income to both parties** — talk to a
CPA before shaking hands.

**Managing and marketing rather than doing manual labor is the correct instinct.** Your time
is worth more directing the vision and filling the classes than swinging a machete. Don't let
anyone talk you out of it.

## 8. Decoupling the two problems

The most useful thing in this document:

> **The business does not need the property to start.**

You can run butterfly-and-bird painting experiences *now* — at existing venues, as pop-ups,
in partnership with Marathon hotels, resorts, and the state parks. That path:

- Builds **documented income**, which is precisely what the mortgage problem requires
- **Proves demand and pricing** with real money before you commit to a mortgage
- Builds the **audience and brand** that make the property viable on day one
- Creates the **track record** that makes a seller, partner, or lender take you seriously

Right now you have one problem stacked on another: no financing without income, no income
without the business, no business (you assumed) without the property. **Starting the business
without the property breaks the stack.**

## 10. On Chris as co-buyer and agent

- **Dual role is legal in Florida but must be disclosed in writing.** Fine, just paper it.
- **The commission is a real benefit** — buyer-side commission on a $750k deal could be
  $15–25k, potentially returned as a closing credit. Legitimate reason to involve him.
- **But this is the wrong first transaction.** Distressed, uninhabitable, entitlement-complex,
  mixed-use, in a market with zero building allocations. This deal would stretch a 20-year
  Keys veteran. Not a knock on him — it's the deal, not the person.
- **Recommendation:** Chris stays in, under an experienced Keys broker, plus a **Keys land use
  attorney**. Non-negotiable given the building rights exposure.
- **Get a broker opinion of value from someone with no stake** — not from Chris, since he'd be
  both agent and co-buyer.
- **Paper the partnership before you make an offer.** Ownership split, who contributes what,
  what happens if one wants out, who lives there and whether they pay rent to the partnership,
  how the business relates to the real estate, what happens on death or disagreement. Two
  people with unstable incomes co-buying a distressed property is exactly where friendships
  and finances break. Written agreement first. Every time.

## Bottom line

The business idea is good. The property is compelling. **The financing is the binding
constraint, and it is not close** — you are at roughly a third of the income a conventional
purchase requires, and this particular property can't be conventionally financed anyway.

That doesn't kill it. It changes the order of operations:

1. **Option the property** if the seller will do it, to stop the clock.
2. **Ask about seller financing** — the highest-probability acquisition path.
3. **Start the business without the property**, this year.
4. **Verify the building rights** (see `due-diligence.md`) — they're the whole thesis.
5. **Revisit acquisition in 18–24 months** with real revenue, real books, and a real track
   record.

You are not too far away to do this. You are too far away to do it *this way*.
