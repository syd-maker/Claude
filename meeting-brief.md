# Property Walkthrough — Meeting Brief

**For today's meeting with the owners.** Read on the way. The vision is §1, the questions are
§4, and the one thing you must not forget is §5.

---

## 1. The vision, in words you can say out loud

> *"We want to make this a lush, living garden — somewhere full of birds and butterflies that
> we actually live in and take care of, and eventually share with people. Right now it's
> fire ants and chicken feed. We want it to be the most peaceful corner of Marathon."*

That's it. Don't pitch a business. **Pitch stewardship.**

She is choosing who gets her land. The thing that wins is not a number — it's her believing
you'll love it. So talk about:

- **What stays.** Every mature tree you can keep. Ask her which ones mean something to her
- **The chickens.** They stay. A proper coop, and she's welcome anytime
- **What it becomes.** Native plants, birds, butterflies, shade, quiet
- **That you'll live there.** You're not flipping it. You're moving in

### Say this version, not the business-plan version
| Say | Don't say |
|---|---|
| "a garden we live in and share" | "a painting experience business" |
| "we want it peaceful" | "revenue projections" |
| "we'd keep the chickens" | "we'd need to relocate the chickens" |
| "what would you want preserved?" | "the buildings need to come down" |

---

## 2. Garden-first is the right call — and it's a better plan

Dropping the business to phase two is genuinely smarter, for a reason that has nothing to do
with ambition:

**The garden takes the longest to grow and is the only thing money can't accelerate.** Plant it
first, let it mature for three or four years while you simply live there, and by the time you
*want* to invite people in, you have something real instead of a new landscape job.

It also strips out the hardest parts of the earlier plan: no commercial parking requirement, no
food service permits, no public-attraction insurance load, no DOH anything. It's a home with a
remarkable yard and one rental — a normal purchase, not a development project.

**The honest trade:** without the business, the property's income is just the one rental
(~$52,900/yr net), so the mortgage is no longer self-funding from the property alone. You'd be
carrying it on your own income plus the rental. That's workable, and it's a very different
risk profile — simpler, slower, far less to go wrong. Worth modeling properly after the
meeting, not before.

## 3. What to look at while you walk it

**Structures** — photograph everything, inside and out. You'll want this later for the
exemption question, and for the before-and-after.
- Any red tags, condemnation notices, or stop-work orders posted?
- Do the buildings look like they were *permitted* as dwellings — kitchens, bathrooms, meter
  bases? Or more like sheds that got lived in?
- How many separate electric meters? **Meter count is a clue to how many dwelling units the
  city recognizes**

**The yard**
- Which trees are mature natives worth saving? Gumbo limbo, seagrape, mahogany, thatch palm
- Where does water sit after rain? Low spots change the whole garden plan
- **Where is the sun?** Note which parts get full sun — that's where butterflies will live
- How bad are the fire ants, and where? Count mounds roughly
- How deep is the chicken-area contamination? Feed storage, droppings, how long it ran

**Infrastructure**
- Is it on **central sewer** or septic? This matters a lot for cost
- Water meter, electric service size
- Where does the driveway come in, and is there room for parking

**The lots**
- Where is the actual lot line between them? Ask her to point
- Anything encroaching from neighbors — fences, sheds, trees

## 4. Questions to ask her, in order

**About the rights (the most valuable thing on this property):**
1. "Do you know how many building rights or ROGO exemptions the property has?"
2. "Has anyone from the city ever given you anything in writing about that?"
3. "Were all the structures originally permitted as homes?"

**About the property:**
4. "How long have you owned it? Has it been in the family?"
5. "Is there a survey anywhere? Old permits, plans, paperwork?"
6. "Has anything flooded, and how high did it get?"
7. "Is there a mortgage on it currently?" *(Matters enormously for seller financing — a
   due-on-sale clause changes everything. Ask it casually.)*
8. "Has it been listed before? Any offers fall through, and why?"

**About her:**
9. **"What would you want to see happen here?"** — the most important question you'll ask.
   Then be quiet and listen
10. "Is there anything you'd want to keep, or anything that matters to you about the place?"
11. "What's your timeline? Is there a reason to move soon or not?"

---

## 5. The one thing not to forget

> ## Do not let anyone demolish, clear, or "clean up" a structure until the building rights are confirmed in writing.
>
> Marathon ran out of market-rate building rights in late 2025. The rights attached to those
> buildings are worth more than the dirt, and they're attached to the *structures existing.*
> Tear one down before the city confirms it in writing, and the right can vanish — permanently,
> unbuyable at any price.
>
> **If she offers to clear the lot for you as a kindness: thank her, and say you'd rather handle
> it after the paperwork.**

---

## 6. What not to do today

- **Don't name a price.** Not even a range. "We're still figuring out what we can do" is a
  complete sentence
- **Don't negotiate terms.** Seller financing comes up on a later conversation, and only if she
  raises money first. Today is about the place
- **Don't point out flaws to drive the price down.** She loves this property. Criticizing it is
  the fastest way to lose her
- **Don't commit to anything.** Not a number, not a date, not a condition
- **Don't bring up demolition.** Say "replace" or "rebuild" if it comes up at all

**The goal today is one thing: she leaves wanting you to be the one who gets it.** Everything
financial can happen later, and happens more easily once she's decided she likes you.

---

## 7. Fire ants and chicken poop — the practical answer

You named these, so here's the real version.

**Fire ants** are manageable in a butterfly garden, but **only with targeted mound treatment, not
broadcast spraying.** Baits placed at individual mounds get carried into the colony with
minimal exposure to anything else; a broadcast insecticide would kill every butterfly and
caterpillar on the property. Talk to **UF/IFAS Monroe County Extension** — free, expert, and
they'll know what's working locally. This is a solved problem; it just has to be done the right
way.

**The chicken area** needs actual soil testing before you plant anything you'll sit in —
pathogens and nitrogen loading, mostly. Years of droppings and spilled feed also means rodents,
so the feed storage is the thing to deal with first. Remediable, but budget for it rather than
assuming a few truckloads of topsoil will fix it.

Both of these are **ordinary problems with known solutions.** Neither should scare you off the
property, and neither is worth mentioning to her today.

---

## One line to carry in

**You're not buying a lot. You're asking her to hand something she loves to someone who'll love
it too. Act like that, and the rest gets easier.**
