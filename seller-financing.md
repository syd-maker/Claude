# Seller Financing — Structure and Term Sheet

You reached the right conclusion for the right reason: **the structures aren't insurable, so no
conventional lender will touch it.** That's not a workaround, it's the correct read. And the
seller's motivations — keeping it with people she knows, someone who'll look after the chickens
— are worth more here than a higher cash offer from a stranger.

**One reframe: none of this is sneaky.** Seller financing is completely standard, done through
a title company with a recorded mortgage, the same as any other closing. What you want to avoid
is the informal version — a handshake, a side letter, an unrecorded agreement. Those are what
go wrong.

---

## 1. Pick the right structure — this decides whether the plan works at all

| Structure | You get title at closing? | Can you build? | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Purchase-money mortgage** | **Yes** | **Yes** | **This is the one** |
| Contract for deed / land contract | No — seller holds title until paid | No | Avoid |
| Lease-option | No | No | Avoid |
| Lease-purchase | No | No | Avoid |

**Why rent-to-own kills the project:** you cannot pull building permits, demolish structures, or
finance construction on property you don't own. Every rent-to-own variant leaves title with the
seller — which means **you'd be making payments for years while unable to build anything.** The
whole plan stalls.

**A purchase-money mortgage gives you everything you need:** you take title at closing and the
seller takes back a recorded mortgage as security. She's the bank. You own it, you can permit
and build, you build equity from day one, and you can refinance later into a DSCR loan. She's
protected — if you default, she forecloses and gets the property back.

It also feels the same to her as what you described. She receives a monthly payment instead of a
lump sum. That *is* the "slow pay" idea — just structured so it actually works for both of you.

---

## 2. Your strongest argument to her: the tax

This is the part most buyers never mention, and it may be worth more to her than price.

If she sells for cash, **she recognizes the entire capital gain in one tax year.** On a
long-held Keys property that could be an enormous single-year hit, pushing her into the top
capital gains bracket and potentially triggering Medicare IRMAA surcharges.

Under an **installment sale (IRC §453)** — which is the default treatment for seller financing
unless she elects out — she recognizes gain **only as she receives payments**, spread across as
many years as the note runs. That can:

- Keep her in lower capital gains brackets year over year
- Reduce exposure to IRMAA and other income thresholds
- Produce interest income on top of the sale price

**She may well net more after tax from financing you than from a higher all-cash offer.** That's
a real, honest argument — and it's the one to lead with, right after the relationship.

*(Caveat worth knowing: depreciation recapture is recognized in full in the year of sale
regardless. Her CPA needs to run this, not you.)*

## 3. Proposed term sheet

A starting point to bring to her, not a final offer.

| Term | Proposed | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Structure** | Purchase-money mortgage, recorded, title insurance, closed at a title company | You own it and can build |
| **Price** | TBD — after an independent broker opinion of value | Don't name a number before you know the building rights are intact |
| **Down payment** | 5–10% | Realistic for you; enough that she sees commitment |
| **Interest rate** | 5–6% | Below bank rates, but far above what her cash earns sitting still |
| **Years 1–2½: interest-only** | **~$3,000–4,000/mo** | **The key term.** Matches your zero-revenue construction period |
| **Year 3 onward** | Fully amortizing | Starts once the business is actually earning |
| **Balloon** | Year 7–10 | You refinance into a DSCR loan (see `construction-and-carry.md`) |
| **Prepayment** | No penalty | So you can refinance the moment you qualify |
| **Her security** | First mortgage on the property | She's fully protected |

**The interest-only period is the single most important term.** Your carry problem is that you
have no revenue for the first two years. An interest-only start solves it without her
giving anything up — she still gets paid every month, just principal-deferred.

### The non-financial terms — where you win this
These cost you almost nothing and address what she actually cares about:

- **The chickens stay.** A maintained coop, with her welcome to visit any time.
- **Naming or legacy.** The garden, a path, or a bench carries her family's name permanently.
- **A standing invitation.** Free classes, free coffee, for life.
- **Right of first refusal** if you ever sell.
- **She sees the vision first.** Bring drawings, the plant list, the transformation plan. She's
  choosing a steward, not just a buyer — so show her what you'll steward.

## 4. The chickens are an asset, not a problem

Take this seriously rather than nodding along. It matters to her, and there's a real operational
issue underneath.

**The issue:** chicken feed means rodents. Rodents mean you cannot get food service permitted for
a coffee stand. That's a health code reality, not a preference.

**The resolution:** keep chickens in a designated, well-built coop, with **rodent-proof sealed
feed storage**, sited well away from food service and guest areas. That satisfies health code
completely — and honestly, chickens wandering a curated jungle garden is *charmingly on-brand*
for the Keys. Guests would love it.

So you can say yes to her, truthfully, and it costs you a coop and some discipline about feed
storage. That's a very cheap way to win the thing she cares most about.

## 5. Guardrails — do not skip these

- **Hire a Florida real estate attorney.** Not optional. Seller financing done wrong can leave
  her unable to foreclose or collect, with serious legal exposure on both sides.
- **Check for existing liens first.** If she still has a mortgage, a due-on-sale clause could be
  triggered by the transfer, and the deal needs restructuring. **Verify before you propose
  anything.**
- **Dodd-Frank may apply.** It governs seller financing where the buyer occupies as a primary
  residence. There's a one-property-per-12-months exclusion that likely covers her, with
  conditions — no negative amortization, and a fixed rate or one that adjusts no sooner than
  five years. Your attorney confirms this. Getting it wrong is one of the few ways to genuinely
  hurt her, which you don't want.
- **Close at a title company with title insurance.** Recorded mortgage, proper closing, clean
  chain of title. Everything else is how friendships end.
- **Get the exemption letter first.** All of this is moot if the three building rights aren't
  intact. Nothing goes under contract without it.

---

## 6. Why this is the better path anyway

Beyond being the only path available, seller financing is genuinely superior here:

- **Saves roughly two years.** No two-year tax-return requirement means closing in 2027 rather
  than 2029 (`timeline.md`).
- **Interest-only during construction** solves the carry gap that conventional debt can't.
- **Below-market rate** moves straight to your bottom line.
- **Flexible terms** — a bank won't defer principal for your build. A person who likes you will.

The insurance problem you identified is also **temporary.** It exists because the current
structures are uninsurable. Once you demolish and build new elevated construction, the property
becomes insurable and financeable — which is exactly what makes the refinance possible.

---

## Bottom line

Ask for a **purchase-money mortgage with 5–10% down, 5–6% interest, interest-only for the first
two and a half years, amortizing after, balloon at year 7–10.**

Lead with the relationship, then the tax benefit, then the chickens. Bring drawings.
Bring an attorney. Get the exemption letter first.

She wants to hand this to someone who'll love it. **Be the person who shows up with a plan.**
