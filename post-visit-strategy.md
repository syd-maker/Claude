# Post-Visit Strategy

Answers to the questions raised after walking the property.

---

## 1. ❌ "We're guaranteed to flood, so we won't have flood insurance"

**This premise is wrong, and it's load-bearing — so worth correcting first.**

**Flood insurance is available.** Monroe County participates in the NFIP, and coverage is
written on exactly this kind of property every day.

| | |
|---|---|
| **Pre-FIRM Keys homes (pre-1975)** average | **~$3,800/yr** |
| Southern Monroe County average | ~$4,697/yr |
| Coastal VE zone average | ~$8,200/yr |
| **Unincorporated Monroe CRS discount** | **35% off premiums** |
| NFIP coverage cap | $250,000 building / $100,000 contents |

### Two things that work in your favor
**Risk Rating 2.0 prices the individual building**, not the zone letter — distance to water,
first-floor height, **and replacement cost.** A modest, low-value structure gets a modest
premium. The expensive policies are on expensive houses.

**The $250,000 cap is a problem for most Keys properties but probably not for yours.** It's
below Keys rebuild cost generally — but your structures are small and modest. The cap may be
adequate.

### The part to plan around
**You should assume you'll be required to carry it.** It's mandatory with any
federally-regulated mortgage in a flood zone, and a seller carrying the paper would be foolish
not to require it too. Budget it as a fixed annual cost, not an option.

> **So the question isn't "can we get flood insurance." It's "what does it cost on this
> specific building" — and that's one phone call to a Keys insurance agent with the address.**
>
> Make that call before you make an offer. It's free and it's one of the three numbers that
> decides this deal.

## 2. ✅ "Don't put money into the structure, invest in the yard"

**This strategy is sound.** And it's more coherent than you may realize, for a reason worth
naming:

> ### Keeping the structure's value low also keeps the flood premium low.
> Risk Rating 2.0 prices on replacement cost. A minimally-finished building is a cheaper
> building to insure. **Your instinct is self-reinforcing.**

### But one serious caveat about the yard

**A three-foot storm surge is saltwater.** Saltwater kills most ornamentals outright — and a
50-year collector's garden is full of exotics that have no salt tolerance at all. The Hawaiian
hibiscus, the tropicals, much of the collection. Irma presumably already proved this.

**Here's the synthesis that matters:**

> ## The native-plant butterfly strategy *is* the flood-resilience strategy.
>
> Keys natives — gumbo limbo, seagrape, buttonwood, firebush, cocoplum, thatch palm — evolved
> with salt spray and periodic inundation. **They come back.** The exotic collector plants
> don't.
>
> So planting for butterflies and planting for hurricane survival are **the same project.**
> That's what makes the garden the right place to put money: it's the asset that recovers.

**Design rule:** natives as the permanent bones of the garden. Exotics only as replaceable
accents, and ideally in pots you can move. Never let an irreplaceable thing be salt-vulnerable.

## 3. 🎯 "I can't be the operator" — then don't be. Lease it.

You've said this twice now, so take yourself seriously. The answer isn't to find motivation.
**It's to structure the business so it doesn't need you.**

### Coffee: lease the spot, don't run the cart
Rent the pad to an **existing licensed operator** who already has a cart, a commissary
agreement, and a food manager certification. You collect rent or a revenue share.

| | You operate | You lease the spot |
|---|---|---|
| Licensing | Yours | **Theirs** |
| Staffing | Yours | **Theirs** |
| Commissary | Yours | **Theirs** |
| Your time | Daily | **~Zero** |
| Revenue | Higher | Lower but passive |

**Lower revenue, near-zero burden. For someone who doesn't want to run it, that's the right
trade.**

### 🌟 Plants: the seller is your operator

This is the best idea to come out of today.

**He has the plant connections. He knows every specimen. He has the Homestead relationships. He
had the permit. And he desperately wants this garden to survive.**

**Propose that he keeps selling plants from the property after the sale.** Revenue share, or he
pays a small amount, or it's simply part of the deal.

What this solves, all at once:
- ✅ Your operator problem — he runs it, not you
- ✅ His emotional attachment — he isn't losing the garden, he's keeping a role in it
- ✅ His income need — he said he needs monthly payments; this is another stream
- ✅ Your knowledge gap — you inherit fifty years of plant knowledge instead of guessing
- ✅ **It is a powerful negotiating sweetener that costs you almost nothing**

> **Say this one out loud to him.** *"What if you kept selling plants out of here? We'd love to
> keep the garden going and we don't know a fraction of what you do."*
>
> That may do more for this negotiation than any number you could name.

## 4. 🔧 The leaks — your worry is right, and the answer is sequencing

> *"I don't want to fix something that gets ruined by the leaking in the back of the house and
> the roof leak."*

**Correct instinct. The rule is absolute:**

> ## Envelope first. Nothing inside gets finished until the building is watertight.

**Order of operations:**
1. **Roof** — diagnose and fix
2. **Back wall leak** — diagnose and fix (you noted the wall needs sealing)
3. *Only then* anything interior

**And note:** the pretty ceiling you liked is precisely what a roof leak destroys. **The ceiling
is an argument for fixing the roof first**, not an argument for starting inside.

### Do this before closing, not after
"The roof leaks somewhere" could be a $3,000 repair or a $30,000 replacement. Same for the wall.
**Get both diagnosed during due diligence** — a roofer and a competent GC, two hours of their
time — because the answer should affect your price.

**That's also leverage.** Documented defects are the most legitimate reason to negotiate price
there is, and it's a conversation about facts rather than opinion.

## 5. 💰 The down payment standoff — trade price for terms

He's locked on a high down payment. **The counter is not a higher down payment.**

> ## "We can't do half down. But what if we paid more in total, over a longer period, at a monthly number that works for you?"

**Sellers who want cash will usually trade total price for structure.** And you already know he
named a **monthly payment figure** — which tells you what he actually wants is **income, not a
lump sum.** He's walked himself toward this.

### What to find out before countering
1. **His required monthly number** — the single most important unknown
2. **The tenant's rent** — it offsets that monthly directly, and you don't know it
3. **Whether there's a mortgage** — determines whether seller financing is even possible

### The stack that could cover the down payment
| Source | Note |
|---|---|
| Parents' partial loan | **Document it as a written note with terms.** Not a vague family arrangement — it affects future refinancing, and papering it protects the relationship |
| Tenant's rent | Reduces what the monthly needs to be |
| Plant sales | $25–69 license. Could start almost immediately |
| Price-for-terms trade | Costs you more over time, nothing today |

- **Landscaping company** — clearing, hauling, hardscape

## 7. The honest summary

**What's genuinely good:** a 50-year mature garden with ponds, a name and a story, a sitting
tenant paying from day one, city water, city sewer, a prior sell permit, plant connections, and
a nursery business that costs $69 to license.

**What's genuinely hard:** a seller anchored on a big down payment, two active leaks, flood
exposure, buildings that may or may not be salvageable, and **no operator** — including you.

**The shape of a deal that works:**
- Price traded upward for a low down payment and a monthly number he likes
- Tenant's rent offsets the carry from day one
- Fix the envelope, furnish lightly, live in it
- Invest in the garden — in **natives**, which survive the floods
- **He keeps selling plants.** You lease the coffee spot to someone else later
- Nothing irreplaceable gets built until the flood question is answered in writing

**Three numbers still missing, and all three are one phone call each:**
1. **Flood insurance quote** on this specific address
2. **His required monthly payment**
3. **The tenant's current rent**

Get those three before anyone writes anything up.

## 8. 🔵 DECISION: no flood insurance at the outset

**Owner's decision, made with the math in hand.** This section is about executing it well
rather than relitigating it.

### 🔴 The thing that actually makes this dangerous — and it isn't the weather

> ## If the note requires insurance and you don't carry it, you are in default — flood or no flood.
>
> That's a breach of covenant. He could accelerate or foreclose **on that alone, with clear
> skies**, and it is a far more likely way to lose this property in year one than a storm is.

**Non-negotiable:** the note and mortgage must **explicitly** say no flood insurance is
required, or carry a written waiver. Not a verbal understanding, not "he said it's fine." Your
attorney drafts it. **If he won't waive it in writing, you carry the policy** — being quietly
out of covenant is the worst of both worlds.

### ⚠️ Flood, wind, and liability are three different things
Make sure you're dropping only the one you mean to:

| Coverage | What it does | Should you drop it? |
|---|---|---|
| **Flood** (NFIP/private) | Rising water, storm surge | **Your decision — dropping** |
| **Wind / hurricane** | Wind damage, roof loss | **No.** Far more frequent than flood here, and a note will almost certainly require it |
| **Liability / hazard** | Someone hurt on the property | **No.** Especially with a tenant and eventually visitors |

Dropping flood is one decision. **Keep the other two.** In Florida windstorm is often a separate
policy or written through Citizens — confirm what you actually have.

### ⏰ You can't buy it when a storm is coming
**NFIP has a 30-day waiting period.** "I'll get it if the season looks bad" does not work — by
the time a storm is named it's far too late.

**The one exception: the waiting period is waived when a policy is bought in connection with a
loan closing.** So **closing is the single cheapest moment to start coverage** if you ever
change your mind. Worth knowing before you pass on it.

### 💡 Close after hurricane season — free risk reduction
**Hurricane season is June 1 – November 30.** Closing in **December** hands you roughly six
months of exposure-free ownership to clean up, plant, and get established before you're
carrying real risk uninsured.

That costs nothing and it's pure downside reduction. **Target a post-season closing.**

### ⚖️ This changes the price-for-terms trade
Earlier the recommendation was to trade a **higher total price** for a **lower down payment.**
Uninsured, that logic weakens:

> **Your entire exposure is the size of the note.** A bigger note means a bigger hole if the
> building is destroyed.
>
> So uninsured, **keep the note as small as you can** — which argues for negotiating the price
> *down*, not accepting a higher price for easier terms.

Pick one strategy and be consistent: **cheap insurance plus a bigger note**, or **no insurance
plus the smallest note possible.** Mixing them gives you the worst of each.

### ✅ What makes the decision hold up
- **Don't put money into the structure.** This was already the plan; it's now load-bearing. Every
  dollar in the building is a dollar uninsured
- **Furnish cheap and replaceable.** Second-hand, nothing precious, nothing irreplaceable
- **Store anything valuable above ~4 feet**, or somewhere movable — tools, equipment, potted
  specimens
- **Keep the irreplaceable things off the property entirely**
- **Build the cash reserve instead of paying premiums.** The ~$2,470/yr you're not spending
  should go into a dedicated storm fund, not general cash flow. **Self-insuring only works if
  you actually self-insure**
- **Invest in natives** — the garden is your real asset and natives survive salt. See §2

### 🔁 Revisit the decision when
- You invest real money in a structure
- The note grows, or you take on additional debt
- You build anything new
- A tenant's livelihood depends on the building
- You've built meaningful equity worth protecting
- **Any time you'd struggle to walk away from a total loss**
