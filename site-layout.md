# Site Layout Plan — Design Logic

Zone-level planning, not a surveyed site plan. Real dimensions come after the survey, but the
*logic* below holds regardless of how the lots measure out — and it's what you hand a designer.

---

## 1. The design tension you have to solve first

**Butterflies need sun. A jungle is shade.**

This is the single most important thing on this page. A densely canopied "curated jungle" will
be beautiful and will have **almost no butterflies** — they need sunlight to warm their flight
muscles, and they feed in open, sunny, nectar-rich areas.

**The resolution — and it makes the experience better, not worse:**

> **Shaded jungle corridors that open into sunny nectar clearings.**

Compression and release. Guests move through cool, dense, green, slightly mysterious paths,
then emerge into bright open clearings full of flowers and butterflies. That contrast is what
makes gardens feel magical — and it's also just correct habitat design.

The jungle is the *journey*. The clearings are the *destination*. Paint in the clearings.

---

## 2. Three zones, front to back

### Zone A — Street edge (public, commercial)
Your frontage on 74th is the billboard. Design rule: **show green, but don't reveal the garden.**
A glimpse creates curiosity; a full view removes the reason to come in.

- **Coffee kiosk** right at the frontage — visible, approachable, no commitment to walk up
- **A green wall or tall planting** screening the garden behind it
- **One framed view** — a single gap in the planting showing a slice of what's back there
- Parking (count driven by code — confirm early, it constrains everything)
- ADA path of travel from parking to all commercial areas — legally required, design it in
- Restrooms

### Zone B — The garden (the experience)
- **Threshold** — a narrow, shaded, compressed entry. A gate, an arch, a tunnel of green.
  This is the moment the street disappears
- **Jungle corridors** — winding, shaded, dense. Never let guests see the whole site at once
- **Nectar clearings** — sunny, open, flower-packed. This is where butterflies live and where
  people paint
- **Painting pavilion** — in or beside the main clearing. Covered, open-sided, fans
- **Water feature** — a shallow stream or basin. Birds bathe, butterflies "puddle" on wet sand
- **Return loop** — guests exit on a *different* path than they entered. Twice the garden for
  the same footprint, and it feels bigger
- **Exit through the kiosk** — last chance for coffee, prints, merchandise

### Zone C — Private (back of site)
- **Your residence** — furthest back, visually buffered by planting
- **The duplex** — separate access and parking so guests never cross paths with tenants
- **Service yard** — trash, utilities, tools, deliveries. Screened, off the guest route
- **Chicken coop + sealed feed storage** — see §4

## 3. Design moves that cost nothing and matter enormously

**Use the elevated buildings.** FEMA requires new construction to be elevated, which hands you
covered, shaded, open ground-level space underneath. That is your **rain-day painting pavilion,
event space, and shaded seating** at near-zero marginal cost. Design for it deliberately rather
than treating it as leftover space.

**Plant a windbreak.** The Keys are windy and butterflies won't fly in strong wind. A dense
hedge on the windward side of your nectar clearings is the difference between butterflies and
no butterflies.

**Never reveal the whole site.** Curves, screens, and layered planting make 1.5 lots feel like
five acres. A straight sightline to the back fence makes it feel small.

**Put host plants where chewing is a feature.** Caterpillars destroy their host plants — that's
the point. Site passionvine and milkweed slightly off the main path with an interpretive sign
("this damage means it's working"), and keep the nectar plants pristine and front-of-house.

**Design the photo.** One spot where the light, the flowers, and the framing are perfect. Every
guest will post it. That's your marketing engine, and it's free.

## 4. The chickens — where they go and why it matters

Your deal term with the seller, and a real health-code constraint.

- **Sited far from food service and guest dining.** Non-negotiable for permitting
- **Sealed, rodent-proof feed storage.** Chicken feed attracts rats; rats mean no food service
  permit. This is the specific thing that matters
- **Downwind of the garden and pavilion**
- **But visible from a path** — if guests can see the chickens from a distance, they become a
  charming feature rather than a hidden liability. Very on-brand for the Keys

## 5. Constraints to confirm before any design work

| Constraint | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| **Required parking count** | Frequently the binding limit on how many guests you can serve — this caps class size, not the pavilion |
| Setbacks front/side/rear | Determines the actual buildable envelope |
| ADA path of travel | Legally required to all commercial areas |
| FEMA base flood elevation | Sets how high the buildings sit |
| Sun path and prevailing wind | Where the nectar clearings must go |
| Existing mature trees | Anything established is worth years — survey and keep what you can |

**Get the survey before you spend a dollar on design.** Everything above is arrangement logic;
the survey turns it into a plan.

### The middle path
A **shallow flowing stream** through the garden runs **$15,000–50,000**, needs no DOH permit
because nobody swims in it, costs a few hundred a year to run — and actively earns its keep:
**birds bathe in it and butterflies puddle at its edges.**

If you want a lazy river because *you* want to float at your own house, that's a real and
legitimate want. Build it in Zone C behind your residence, private, out of profit, years from
now — where it never touches the business's insurance or the DOH's jurisdiction. That version
skips the $31,800 compliance bill and most of the annual cost.
