# Seller Financing vs. a Normal Mortgage — Plain English

**Yes, "seller financing" is the correct term.** You'll also hear owner financing, seller
carryback, holding paper, or a purchase-money mortgage. Same thing.

---

## The one-sentence version

> **Normal mortgage:** a bank hands the seller a pile of cash, and you owe the bank.
> **Seller financing:** there is no pile of cash — the seller lets you pay her over time, and
> she holds a lien on the property until you're done.

That's the whole idea. Everything below is detail.

---

## What actually happens on closing day

### With a bank
1. You show up with **$150,000** cash (20% down)
2. The bank wires **$600,000** to the seller
3. The seller walks away with **$750,000, all at once**
4. The deed goes in your name
5. The bank records a mortgage against the property as security
6. You pay the bank **$3,892/month for 30 years**

### With seller financing
1. You show up with **$56,250** cash (7.5% down)
2. **Nobody wires anything.** There is no bank in the room
3. The seller gets her **$56,250**, and a signed promissory note for the rest
4. **The deed still goes in your name** — this is the important part
5. **The seller records a mortgage against the property** — the exact same kind of document a
   bank would file
6. You pay **her** every month instead of a bank

Steps 4 and 5 are identical in both cases. The only difference is *who* is owed the money.

## Side by side

| | Bank mortgage | Seller financing |
|---|---|---|
| **Cash you need at closing** | ~$172,500 | **~$67,500** |
| Down payment | 20% ($150,000) | 7.5% ($56,250) |
| Closing costs | ~3% ($22,500) | ~1.5% ($11,250) |
| **Income you must document** | **$150,000–179,000/yr** | **Whatever the seller accepts** |
| Two years of tax returns | Required | Not required |
| Credit score minimum | Yes | Seller's call |
| Appraisal | Required | Optional |
| **Insurance binder required** | **Yes — this kills your deal** | **No** |
| **Habitable structure required** | **Yes — this also kills your deal** | **No** |
| Interest rate | ~6.75% | Negotiable — 5–6% |
| Interest-only start | Almost never | **Yes, if she agrees** |
| Time to close | 45–60 days | 2–3 weeks |
| Who you pay | The bank | Her |
| Who holds the deed | **You** | **You** |
| If you stop paying | Bank forecloses | She forecloses |

**Two rows decide this whole thing:** the bank requires an insurable, habitable structure. Your
buildings are neither. That's not a hurdle you can hustle past — it's a hard no. The seller has
no such requirement, because she's not protecting an underwriting department. She's protecting
herself, and she already knows exactly what condition the property is in.

## The money, over time

### What you'd pay her

| Period | Payment |
|---|---|
| Down payment at closing | $56,250 |
| **Months 1–30 (construction, no revenue)** | **$3,180/mo — interest only** |
| Month 31 onward | $4,082/mo — principal + interest |
| Year 7–10 | Balloon — you refinance into a real loan and pay her off |

### Why the interest-only start matters so much

During your first 30 months you have **zero revenue** — permits, demolition, construction.

| First 30 months of payments | |
|---|---|
| Bank | $116,748 |
| Seller (interest-only) | $95,391 |
| **Saved** | **$21,357** |

And that's before the $105,000 less you need at closing. Combined, seller financing keeps
roughly **$126,000 in your pocket** during the period when you have no income at all.

## What's in it for her

This is not charity, and you shouldn't pitch it as a favor.

| | Cash sale | Financing you |
|---|---|---|
| Money in year 1 | $750,000 | $56,250 + payments |
| **Capital gains tax** | **Entire gain in one year** | **Spread over years** |
| Interest income | None | ~$38,000/yr at the start |
| Who ends up with it | Whoever bids most | **Someone she chose** |
| The chickens | Gone | Cared for |

Her money keeps earning 5.5% secured against real estate she knows intimately — better than
a savings account, and she can foreclose and take it back if you fail.

The tax piece is the real argument: a cash sale means one enormous capital-gains year. Spreading
it over a decade may leave her with **more after tax**, even at a lower price.

---

## The honest downsides

Not a free lunch. Three real risks:

**1. Your payment is higher later.** $4,082/mo vs. the bank's $3,892 — because you put less down,
you're borrowing more. That's the trade for needing $105k less upfront.

**2. The balloon is a real deadline.** At year 7–10 you owe the remaining balance in full. The
plan is to refinance into a DSCR loan once the buildings are new and insurable and the business
has books. **If that refinance doesn't happen, you lose the property.** This is the single
biggest risk in the structure, and it's why the business actually has to work.

**3. What if she dies?** The note becomes an asset of her estate, and her heirs become your
lender. They may be less flexible than she is. **The note must address succession explicitly** —
that the terms survive, and that heirs can't demand early payoff. Your attorney handles this.
Don't leave it out because it's an awkward conversation.

---

## The bottom line

Seller financing isn't a lesser version of a mortgage. **For this specific property it's the
only version that exists**, because no bank will lend against uninsurable buildings.

You need **$67,500 instead of $172,500** to get in the door, you pay **$3,180/month instead of
$3,892** while you build, and she likely nets more after tax while handing her land to someone
who'll love it.

The catch is the balloon. You have 7–10 years to make the business real enough to refinance.
Everything else in this plan is about making sure that happens.
