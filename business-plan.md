## 1. The concept in one paragraph

Create Florida Keys is a destination art experience in Marathon: guests paint butterflies
and birds while sitting inside a living butterfly and bird garden, surrounded by the actual
subjects. A coffee stand fronting 74th Street serves as the storefront, the impulse-traffic
capture point, and the marketing engine — it tells passers-by that something beautiful is
back here. Behind the commercial frontage, the same parcel carries residential units: the
owner's permanent residence plus income-producing rentals that stabilize the business
through the Keys' seasonal swings.

Three revenue lines, deliberately layered by margin and risk:

| Line | Role | Margin | Seasonality |
|---|---|---|---|
| Painting experience | Profit core | High | Peaks Dec–Apr |
| Coffee stand | Funnel + foot traffic | Medium | Year-round, weather-linked |
| Residential rental | Baseload / debt service | Medium | Flat, low risk |
| Garden itself | The *reason* the other three work | n/a | The moat |

The garden is not a revenue line. It is the differentiator that lets you charge a premium
for the other three, and it is the hardest thing for a competitor to copy — it takes years
to grow.

## 2. The critical path issue: your three building rights

**This section matters more than anything else in the plan.**

Marathon does not allow you to simply build. The city rations residential construction
through the **Building Permit Allocation System (BPAS)** — the local version of the Keys'
ROGO. Historically the city issued roughly 30 residential allocations a year, split about
80% market-rate / 20% affordable. As of late 2025, reporting indicates Marathon has
**exhausted its standard market-rate building rights**, with any further awards coming from
unconventional sources.

Consequences, in order of importance:

### 2.1 The rights are probably worth more than the land

An existing lawfully-established dwelling unit carries a **BPAS/ROGO exemption** — the right
to rebuild that unit without competing for a new allocation. In a market where the queue is
empty, three of those are extraordinarily valuable. Transfers of building rights (TBRs)
between sites are possible in Marathon and carry an administrative transfer fee reported at
**$40,000**, *on top of* whatever the right itself sells for. **`[ASSUMPTION]`** Get a
current price quote from a Keys land broker — this single number reframes the purchase.

### 2.2 Do not demolish anything until the exemptions are confirmed in writing

You described the buildings as unlivable. That is a red flag, not for the buildings, but for
the *rights attached to them*. Exemptions depend on the units being lawfully established and
recognized in the city's records. If a structure was condemned, red-tagged, permitted as
something other than a dwelling, or lapsed through abandonment, the exemption can evaporate —
and once it is gone it cannot be bought back at any reasonable price.

> **Action, before closing, and before a single structure is touched:** obtain a written
> **BPAS/ROGO exemption determination** (sometimes called a letter of development rights)
> from Marathon Planning confirming exactly how many dwelling units the property is entitled
> to rebuild. Make the purchase contract contingent on it.

This is the highest-leverage move available to you in this entire deal.

### 2.3 Three rights is not a fourplex

You mentioned a fourplex. Three building rights means **three dwelling units**. If you live
in one, that leaves **two rental units**, not four. The unit math:

| Scenario | Units | Your home | Income units | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **A. Build to rights** | 3 | 1 | 2 | Cleanest, cheapest, no allocation risk |
| **B. Add workforce units** | 4–6 | 1 | 3–5 | Uses the affordable/workforce pool, which still has supply when market-rate does not. Cost: deed restriction, rent capped to AMI, income-qualified tenants |
| **C. Buy a 4th right (TBR)** | 4 | 1 | 3 | $40k transfer fee **plus** market price of the right. Most expensive path |

**Recommendation: model Scenario A as the base case and Scenario B as the upside.**
Scenario B deserves serious attention — Marathon has an acute workforce housing shortage,
the affordable pool is where allocations still exist, and a project that houses local workers
buys you enormous goodwill with the same city staff and council who will review your
commercial use. That goodwill is a real asset when you are asking for a coffee stand and a
public art attraction in a mixed-use district.

### 2.4 Commercial floor area is allocated separately

The painting studio and coffee stand are **non-residential** floor area, governed by its own
allocation pool, separate from the residential rights. Confirm available non-residential
floor area for the parcel with Planning. Do not assume the residential rights cover it.

## 3. The butterfly attraction: an open garden, not a butterfly house

There is a fork here with a very large cost and timeline difference, and I think the answer
is clearly one side of it.

**Option 1 — Open native butterfly and bird garden (recommended).** You plant host plants and
nectar plants for locally-native South Florida species and the wild butterflies and birds
come to you. No enclosure, no purchased stock, no shipping. This requires **no USDA or FDACS
permitting**, because you are not importing, moving interstate, or releasing anything. Cost
is landscaping cost. Timeline is a growing season.

**Option 2 — Enclosed butterfly house with purchased pupae.** Triggers federal **USDA APHIS
PPQ 526** permitting for interstate movement and release of insects, plus a **FDACS**
application, plus a containment-facility inspection of the structure. Monarch movement is
specifically restricted — APHIS does not permit interstate movement across the continental
divide for release, and only Atlantic-flyway monarchs may be shipped into Florida.

**Go with Option 1.** It is faster, cheaper, permit-free, ecologically honest, and — for a
painting experience — arguably better product. Guests paint wild butterflies that chose to be
there. That is a much better story than a netted enclosure, and it is genuinely rarer.

Design the garden around species that reliably show up in the Middle Keys and that are
*paintable* — large, slow, high-contrast, and willing to perch. Work with a Keys native-plant
nursery and, if you can, the Florida Keys Audubon chapter. **`[ASSUMPTION]`** A native-plant
landscape designer's plan is a line item you should price early; it drives the whole guest
experience.

The bird side is largely the same logic: water features, native berry and seed plants, feeders,
and structure. Migratory birds through the Keys in spring and fall are a genuine seasonal draw
and could anchor a shoulder-season program when tourist volume dips.

## 4. The site as you described it

| What you told me | What it means | What to verify |
|---|---|---|
| Three building rights | Three rebuildable dwelling units — the core asset | Written exemption letter from Planning |
| One full lot + a half lot with a home | Likely 2+ separate parcels | Pull parcel IDs / RE numbers from Monroe County Property Appraiser; confirm lot lines, whether aggregation is required or harmful |
| None of the buildings livable | Rebuild, not renovate | See FEMA 50% rule below |
| Commercial street | Enables the studio and coffee stand | Confirm the actual zoning district and that "art instruction studio" and "food/beverage service" are permitted uses, not conditional |
| Infestation, chickens, chicken feed, weeds | Site remediation cost + timeline | Pest assessment; soil testing before you plant a garden guests will sit in |
| "A lot of land" | Room for garden + parking + units | Survey; confirm parking count required for commercial use — this often constrains Keys projects more than building area does |

### 4.1 The FEMA 50% rule — why "replace the buildings" is likely correct

In the Keys, if the cost of improving a structure exceeds 50% of that structure's market
value, the entire building must be brought up to current flood code, including elevation.
Buildings that are already unlivable will blow through 50% instantly. So your instinct to
replace rather than repair is almost certainly right — but sequence it correctly: **secure
the rights first (§2.2), then demolish.**

New construction will be elevated, which is a cost driver but also creates usable covered
ground-level space. In a garden business, elevated buildings with shaded, open ground level
underneath are a gift: that is your rain-day painting pavilion, your event space, and your
shaded seating, at little marginal cost.

### 4.2 The mess is a marketing asset

The chickens, the feed, the weeds, the infestation — that is a transformation story, and
transformation stories are the single most reliable organic content format there is.
Document the site *now*, before you touch anything: photos, video, drone if you can. A
two-year before-and-after of a trashed lot becoming a butterfly garden will do more for your
launch than any ad spend. Start the account before you own the property.

## 5. Revenue model

### 5.1 Painting experience (profit core)

The product: a 2–2.5 hour guided painting session in the garden, all materials included,
guests leave with a finished canvas of a butterfly or bird they were sitting next to.

| Variable | Base case | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Ticket price | $95 | Keys tourist market supports a premium over mainland paint-and-sip ($45–65) because of the setting |
| Seats per session | 14 | Garden pavilion capacity |
| Sessions per day | 2 | Morning + late afternoon (butterfly activity + light are both better) |
| Operating days, high season (Dec–Apr) | 5/week | |
| Operating days, low season (May–Nov) | 3/week | |
| Load factor, high season | 70% | |
| Load factor, low season | 40% | |
| Materials cost per guest | $15 | Canvas, paint, brush amortization |
| Instructor cost | Owner-led year 1 | Then ~$45/session-hour |

Add-ons worth modeling separately: private/group bookings (bachelorette, corporate retreat,
wedding party) at a premium, which in tourist markets often become the highest-margin segment;
and a kids/family session at a lower price point in the slow midday window.

### 5.2 Coffee stand (funnel)

Its job is not primarily profit. It is (a) a legal, visible, street-facing reason for people
to stop, (b) the conversion point where a walk-up becomes a painting booking, and (c) the
thing that makes the property legible from 74th Street.

Design it so **every** coffee transaction exposes the customer to the painting offer — the
garden visible from the counter, a booking QR on the cup sleeve, a "your coffee is free with
a class booking" hook.

**`[ASSUMPTION]`** $7 average ticket, 40 tickets/day, ~70% gross margin on beverage.
Requires a Florida food service license and health inspection; a mobile/kiosk build is
dramatically cheaper and faster to permit than a built-out café. **Start with a kiosk.**

### 5.3 Residential income (baseload)

Two units under Scenario A. Three options, and I'd push you toward the boring one:

| Option | Gross | Risk | Compatibility with your life |
|---|---|---|---|
| Long-term rental | Lowest | Lowest | **High** — quiet, stable, invisible |
| Vacation rental (7-night min) | Highest | Licensing, management burden, guest churn | **Low** — see below |
| Deed-restricted workforce | Low, capped to AMI | Lowest | High, plus unlocks Scenario B allocations |

Marathon requires a **Vacation Rental Property License** for stays of 7–28 nights (reported
at $500 first year, $250/year after), prohibits rentals under 7 nights, and several
residential zoning districts enforce a 28-day minimum outright. Verify what your specific
district permits.

**My recommendation: do not run vacation rentals here.** You are going to be living on this
property and running a daytime experience business that depends on the garden being calm,
quiet, and beautiful. Weekly guest turnover — luggage, late arrivals, parking, noise, pool
expectations — is directly at odds with that, and it competes for the same parking your
commercial use needs. Long-term or workforce tenancy earns less per door and protects the
thing that actually makes you money.

## 6. Market

The Florida Keys draw roughly **$3.5 billion in annual visitor spending**, supporting 24,000+
jobs, with Monroe County TDC running an ongoing visitor profile study by district. Marathon
sits mid-chain, capturing both destination visitors and the substantial drive-through traffic
on the Overseas Highway heading to and from Key West.

**Your segments, in likely order of value:**

1. **Couples and small groups on vacation, 35–65** — looking for a daytime activity that is
   not a boat, not a bar, and not another snorkel trip. This is an underserved slot in the
   Keys activity market, which skews heavily toward water.
2. **Rain-day and wind-day traffic** — when the water is blown out, every fishing charter and
   snorkel trip in Marathon cancels, and a few hundred people suddenly need something to do.
   An indoor-capable, covered painting experience is *counter-cyclical to weather*. This is a
   genuinely strong position and you should market directly to charter operators and hotel
   concierges as their cancellation fallback.
3. **Locals and seasonal residents** — repeat business, low season revenue, community goodwill.
   Different pricing, different classes, membership or class-pack model.
4. **Birders and nature travelers** — spring and fall migration, high willingness to pay,
   travel in the shoulder seasons you most need to fill.

**Competitive position:** the moat is the garden. Anyone can rent a room and run a paint-and-sip.
Nobody can quickly grow a mature butterfly and bird habitat on a commercial corridor in
Marathon, because the land and the building rights do not exist to be had. Your scarcity
problem in §2 is also your competitive advantage.

## 7. Phasing

Sequenced so that revenue starts before the expensive construction finishes, and so that
nothing irreversible happens before the rights are confirmed.

**Phase 0 — Due diligence (pre-closing).** Exemption letter, zoning confirmation, survey,
soil and pest assessment, insurance quotes. Nothing else matters until §2.2 is answered in
writing. See `due-diligence.md`.

**Phase 1 — Site remediation and garden establishment.** Clear, remediate, test soil, plant.
The garden needs the longest lead time of anything in this plan, so it starts first. Document
everything for content.

**Phase 2 — Coffee kiosk + first classes.** Lowest capital, fastest to revenue. Classes can
run under a shade structure or the covered ground level long before the buildings are finished.
This phase proves demand and pricing with real money before you commit to the full build.

**Phase 3 — Residential construction.** Your residence plus income units. Longest timeline,
largest capital, most financing-dependent.

**Phase 4 — Permanent studio buildout and scale.** Only after Phases 2–3 have produced real
operating data.

## 8. Risks

| Risk | Severity | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|
| **Building rights don't survive DD** | **Critical** | Written exemption letter as a closing contingency. This risk alone justifies walking away |
| Commercial use not permitted as-of-right | High | Confirm district and permitted uses before closing; conditional use = time and uncertainty |
| Insurance cost (wind + flood) | High | Get real quotes during DD, not after. Keys insurance has killed more Keys projects than construction cost |
| Hurricane | High | Elevated new construction, business interruption coverage, off-season capital reserve |
| Construction cost and labor scarcity in the Keys | High | Contingency of 20–30%, not the mainland 10% |
| Soil contamination from years of chicken operation | Medium | Test before planting. Guests will sit in this garden |
| Seasonality | Medium | Locals programming, birder shoulder season, rain-day positioning (§6.2) |
| Owner is the single point of failure | Medium | You are instructor, host, operator, and resident. Plan for a second trained instructor by end of year 1 |
| Parking constrains commercial capacity | Medium | Survey and parking count early — this quietly caps your class size |

## 10. My honest read

The business concept is sound and genuinely differentiated — a nature-based art experience is
counter-cyclical to Keys weather, underserved in an activity market saturated with boats, and
defensible because the garden takes years to replicate.

The risk is not the business. **The risk is the real estate.** You are buying a distressed
property whose principal value is three intangible entitlements that may or may not be intact,
in a market where they can no longer be replaced. Handled correctly — exemption letter first,
demolition second — that scarcity is the best thing about the deal. Handled in the wrong order,
it is a catastrophic and uninsurable loss.

Get the letter. Everything else is solvable.
